# /steer RQ1 ran and came back negative. Let me read the result and the follow-up carefully, because this is the decisive branch.

The frozen rule fired its negative branch, and the student applied it exactly as written, without rationalizing. That's the most important thing in this update.

## What the results establish

**The mechanism we were building the paper on is refuted.** With the polling thread held off the interrupt's CPU (pinned to CPU 10) and the stock driver, **0 of 8 cells stalled**, no cell showed ready-but-unserved work, and every cell drained the full 790k-pps flood. The driver's stop-and-re-arm path fires on every poll there and is harmless. So the "placement-correction path strands work" hypothesis is wrong, and the contract/restart-strategy framing built on it goes too, including the prediction that mlx4 stalls and i40e doesn't.

PERSIST tightened several facts at once:

| Question | Result |
|---|---|
| Is the stall permanent? | **No.** 0 of 10 permanent in the quiet-window arm; every cell eventually recovered. The earlier "dead until reset" was an artifact of short observation windows and resetting before natural recovery |
| Does the default kernel (threaded NAPI off) stall? | **No.** 0 of 10 |
| Is overload required? | **Yes.** 0 of 10 at 525k |
| Is the striding receive queue a trigger? | **No reliable evidence.** Striding on: 1 of 10, against a pre-registered prediction of at least 8. The earlier 8 of 8 was an ordering confound |
| How often does the unpinned configuration stall? | **About 10–20% of flood episodes.** 2 of 10, below the pre-registered 3; 1 of 10 in the striding-on arm |

**What survives, now verified by a validated instrument:** with an *unpinned, migrating* thread under flood, ready receive work really does sit unserved, for up to 86 s of samples within one cell. The phenomenon is real. The explanation we had was not.

## The live lead, and a design flaw to fix before it runs

Every stall so far happened with the thread free to migrate. The first migration cell (MA-1) showed 5 stranded gaps:
- **3 event-silent:** no wake requests at all while work sat ready;
- **1 wake-flowing:** about 44,300 wakes and matching switch-ins, 11 migrations, yet no progress.

That points toward a race at migration moments rather than a design contract. But it's one cell; make no claim from it.

**The migration spec as frozen is underpowered.** It predicts at least 3 of 8 stalls in the migrating arm. At the observed base rate of about 15%, P(at least 3 of 8) is roughly 11%, so the prediction will probably fail even if migration is the cause. Two fixes:
1. **Manipulate migration directly** instead of hoping the scheduler causes it. Add an arm that pins the thread and hops it between two CPUs on a fixed schedule (say, every 100 ms between 10 and 46). If static pinning never stalls and forced hopping stalls often, migration is causal, and the rate becomes controllable.
2. **Size the arms from the base rate.** If the unpinned arm stays in the design, it needs about 30 cells to separate 15% from 0% with any confidence.

Revise and re-freeze the spec before any more cells. It's cheap now and saves a batch that can't answer its own question.

## Honest assessment of where this leaves us

**The current finding:** threaded NAPI with unpinned threads can, under sustained overload, strand ready receive work for tens of seconds in roughly 10–20% of overload episodes. It recovers on its own, pinning prevents it, and the default kernel is unaffected. That's a real operational finding and a kernel bug-report candidate once the race is identified. It is not currently a full paper: the rate is low, it's recoverable, it needs a non-default configuration plus a flood, and the cause is unknown.

**One scenario raises the value substantially:** if the migration race is in the *core* threaded-NAPI code rather than in mlx5, it affects every driver running threaded NAPI. The event-silent gaps lean toward the driver or device (no wake was attempted), and the wake-flowing gap leans the other way, so it's open. Forced migration on a second driver (i40e) would decide it.

Realistic odds of a full paper from here are lower than a week ago, perhaps 20–30%. The workshop-level version, a bug report and fix, is likelier if the race is found.

## Two things to correct now

1. **The disclosure draft is wrong and must not go anywhere.** "Dead until reset" is refuted, the default kernel is clean, and the rate is low. If a report goes out, it goes publicly to netdev as a liveness bug once the mechanism is known, not privately to security@kernel.org.
2. **Governance.** AN-006D says the migration spec awaited PI approval; the next commit froze and ran it under "standing rule". Either is defensible, but the record should say which. I'd approve it, revised for power as above.

## What to tell the student

1. **The negative result is the right outcome of the right test.** Record it as DR-011: the contract mechanism is off the paper.
2. **Revise the migration spec before any more cells:** add the forced-hop arm, size the unpinned arm to about 30 cells or drop it, keep the validated probe, and add traces of re-arm events and the CPU each re-arm runs on around migrations.
3. **Classify every stranded gap** as event-silent, wake-lost (wake issued, thread never runs) or wake-flowing (thread runs, no progress). The first points at the driver or device, the second at the core scheduler path, the third at something else. The classes separate "mlx5 bug" from "core bug".
4. **Time-box it to one week,** with a go/no-go: a migration-causal result plus a gap class pointing at specific code, or we stop and write up what we have as a short note and bug report.
5. **Retire the disclosure draft** (mark it superseded) and update the paper outline to the current facts.

The method is doing its job: it killed our favorite hypothesis cheaply and pointed at a narrower, testable one. What we have is less than we hoped, but it's true, and the next week decides whether the migration race makes it more.
